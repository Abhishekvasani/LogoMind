# Identity Thinking — Starter Set (10 Concepts)

> *Identity Thinking is the volume that connects strategy to execution. It covers the concepts a designer needs to translate Brand DNA into a working identity system — the structural and compositional principles that make logos work as part of a larger whole.*

These are the concepts that sit between the Philosophy principles (what makes a good logo) and the execution tools (how to draw one). They are about *thinking* in identity terms.

---

## RS-LIC-ID-001 — Negative Space

**Definition:** The empty space within or around a mark; the "unmarked" area that gives form its meaning.

**Why It Matters:** Negative space is where the most memorable identity ideas live (FedEx arrow, NBC peacock's whitespace, WWF panda). Mastering negative space is the difference between a flat mark and one that rewards attention.

**Strategic Use:** Hidden meanings, secondary symbols, visual wit, breathing room for the primary form.

**Common Mistakes:**
- Forcing hidden meanings that don't emerge naturally
- Filling all space (eliminating the "breathing" that makes forms clear)
- Making hidden meanings too obvious (patronising) or too obscure (invisible)

**Design Exercise:** Take any letter. Draw it as a solid form. Now draw it as only negative space (the area around it). Compare how each feels.

**Originality Potential:** 🟢 High — well-used negative space is inherently distinctive
**LKG Relationships:** → Simplicity (RS-LIC-PH-003), → Memorability (RS-LIC-PH-006), → Clarity (RS-LIC-PH-004)

## RS-LIC-ID-002 — Visual Hierarchy

**Definition:** The arrangement of elements so the eye knows what to look at first, second, and third.

**Why It Matters:** Without hierarchy, a mark is chaos — every element competes for attention and none wins. With hierarchy, the eye moves gracefully from the dominant element to supporting ones.

**Strategic Use:** Ensuring the primary meaning is read first; secondary meanings layered behind.

**The Three Levels:**
1. **Dominant** — the element the eye sees first (usually the primary symbol or wordmark)
2. **Supporting** — elements that reinforce the dominant (tagline, secondary form)
3. **Background** — elements that provide context (container, texture, colour field)

**Common Mistakes:**
- Making two elements equally dominant (eye can't decide)
- Making the tagline as prominent as the logo
- Too many levels of hierarchy (dilution)

**LKG Relationships:** → Clarity (RS-LIC-PH-004), → Meaning (RS-LIC-PH-001)

## RS-LIC-ID-003 — Grid Systems

**Definition:** An underlying geometric structure that governs the proportions and placement of elements.

**Why It Matters:** Grids provide mathematical harmony — relationships between elements that feel "right" without the viewer knowing why. They make marks feel constructed, not arbitrary.

**Common Grid Types:**
- **Circular grid** — unity, completeness; good for marks needing wholeness
- **Square/rectangular grid** — stability, precision; good for structured marks
- **Golden ratio grid** — natural proportion; good for marks needing organic harmony
- **Triangular grid** — direction, energy; good for dynamic marks
- **Modular grid** — systems thinking; good for marks that extend to families

**Common Mistakes:**
- Applying a grid that doesn't serve the meaning (grid for grid's sake)
- Visible grid lines in the final mark (the grid is a tool, not a feature)
- Inconsistent grid application across the identity system

**LKG Relationships:** → Simplicity (RS-LIC-PH-003), → Consistency (RS-LIC-PH-010)

## RS-LIC-ID-004 — Proportion and Scale

**Definition:** The size relationships between elements within a mark, and the mark's behaviour at different sizes.

**Why It Matters:** Proportion determines whether a mark feels balanced or off. Scale behaviour determines whether it works from favicon to billboard.

**The Scale Test:** A mark must be recognisable at:
- 16px (favicon) — only silhouette matters
- 32px (app icon) — primary form must read
- 100px (business card) — detail becomes visible
- 1000px+ (signage) — detail must hold up, not look pixelated or thin

**Proportion Systems:**
- 1:1 (square) — stability, equality
- 1:1.618 (golden ratio) — natural harmony
- 1:2, 1:3 (strong ratios) — dynamic tension
- Equal-weight elements — balance, formality

**Common Mistakes:**
- Designing only at large scale; failing at favicon
- Inconsistent stroke weights at different sizes
- Proportions that feel arbitrary (no underlying system)

**LKG Relationships:** → Simplicity (RS-LIC-PH-003), → Clarity (RS-LIC-PH-004)

## RS-LIC-ID-005 — Stroke Weight Consistency

**Definition:** The uniform thickness of lines throughout a mark (or the deliberate variation for effect).

**Why It Matters:** Inconsistent stroke weight reads as sloppy or unfinished. Deliberate, consistent stroke weight reads as crafted and professional.

**The Rule:** Within a single mark, all strokes of the same visual "level" should be the same weight. Variation is allowed only when it serves meaning (e.g., heavier weight for the dominant element).

**Common Stroke Languages:**
- **Hairline** — delicate, refined, luxury
- **Medium** — versatile, balanced, professional
- **Bold** — confident, impactful, accessible
- **Variable** — expressive, dynamic (requires deliberate design)

**Common Mistakes:**
- Accidental weight variation (one stroke slightly thicker than another)
- Strokes too thin for small-scale reproduction
- Inconsistent weight across the identity system (logo vs. icons vs. UI)

**LKG Relationships:** → Consistency (RS-LIC-PH-010), → Clarity (RS-LIC-PH-004)

## RS-LIC-ID-006 — Symmetry and Balance

**Definition:** The distribution of visual weight within a mark; whether it mirrors (symmetry) or counterbalances (asymmetry).

**Why It Matters:** Balance determines whether a mark feels stable or dynamic, formal or energetic.

**Types:**
- **Bilateral symmetry** — mirrored halves; stability, formality, authority
- **Radial symmetry** — elements radiating from a centre; unity, completeness
- **Asymmetrical balance** — different elements of equal visual weight; dynamism, modernity
- **Intentional imbalance** — deliberate tension; energy, disruption (use carefully)

**Strategic Choice:**
- Heritage brands → bilateral symmetry
- Modern/tech brands → asymmetrical balance
- Disruptor brands → controlled imbalance
- Community/inclusive brands → radial symmetry

**Common Mistakes:**
- Accidental asymmetry (looks like a mistake, not a choice)
- Over-symmetry (boring, rigid)
- Imbalance that looks unintentional

**LKG Relationships:** → Meaning (RS-LIC-PH-001 — form must serve meaning), → Consistency (RS-LIC-PH-010)

## RS-LIC-ID-007 — Optical Correction

**Definition:** Deliberate adjustments to a mark so it *looks* correct, even when geometrically it isn't.

**Why It Matters:** The eye perceives geometry imperfectly. A perfect circle looks slightly oval. A perfectly centred letter looks off-centre. Optical correction makes marks look *right* by breaking mathematical precision.

**Common Corrections:**
- **Circle in a square** — the circle must extend slightly beyond the square to look equal
- **Letter centring** — letters with curves (O, C) must sit slightly high to look centred
- **Stroke tapering** — strokes meeting at angles need slight thickening at the junction
- **Horizontal vs. vertical** — horizontal strokes must be thinner than vertical to look equal

**Common Mistakes:**
- Trusting geometry over perception ("but it's mathematically centred!")
- Not testing at different sizes (corrections may need adjustment)

**LKG Relationships:** → Clarity (RS-LIC-PH-004), → Craft (the mark feels made by a human, not a machine)

## RS-LIC-ID-008 — Logo Types (Classification)

**Definition:** The major categories of logo type, each with different strengths, weaknesses, and use cases.

**The Five Types:**

| Type | Description | Strengths | Weaknesses |
|------|-------------|-----------|------------|
| **Wordmark** | The brand name as the logo (Coca-Cola, Google) | Maximum name recognition; timeless | Requires strong name; limited visual metaphor |
| **Lettermark** | Abbreviation of the name (IBM, HBO, NASA) | Shortens long names; distinctive | Less expressive; needs recognition to decode |
| **Symbol/Icon** | A graphic mark without text (Apple, Nike) | Universal; language-independent; scales well | Requires brand equity to be recognisable alone |
| **Combination** | Symbol + wordmark together (Adidas, Lacoste) | Best of both; flexible | More complex; needs clear hierarchy |
| **Emblem** | Text enclosed within a shape (Starbucks, Harley-Davidson) | Self-contained; traditional; authoritative | Less flexible; harder to scale; can feel dated |

**Strategic Choice Factors:**
- **Name length** — long names → wordmark or lettermark; short names → any type
- **International audience** — symbol works across languages
- **Brand maturity** — new brands need wordmark/combination; established can use symbol alone
- **Application range** — more applications → simpler/more flexible type

**Common Mistakes:**
- Choosing a symbol-only mark for a new brand (nobody knows who you are)
- Choosing an emblem when flexibility is needed
- Not designing the system (symbol, wordmark, and combination should be designed together)

**LKG Relationships:** → Meaning (RS-LIC-PH-001), → Memorability (RS-LIC-PH-006), → Consistency (RS-LIC-PH-010)

## RS-LIC-ID-009 — Responsive/Variable Identity

**Definition:** An identity system designed to adapt across contexts — different sizes, applications, and media — while maintaining coherence.

**Why It Matters:** Modern brands live across more contexts than ever — favicons, app icons, billboards, social avatars, motion, AR. A fixed mark that can't adapt breaks in modern contexts.

**Approaches:**
- **Responsive simplification** — the mark simplifies at small sizes (detail removed, silhouette preserved)
- **Variable logo system** — multiple versions for different contexts (full combination → symbol → simplified symbol)
- **Adaptive colour** — the mark adapts colour for dark/light contexts
- **Motion identity** — the mark animates between states

**Examples:** Google (responsive D), Heineken (simplified star at small sizes), Mastercard (wordmark optional)

**Common Mistakes:**
- Designing only one version; assuming it works everywhere
- Simplification that loses recognisability
- Inconsistent versions that break coherence

**LKG Relationships:** → Consistency (RS-LIC-PH-010), → Clarity (RS-LIC-PH-004), → Timelessness (RS-LIC-PH-008)

## RS-LIC-ID-010 — The Identity System

**Definition:** The complete set of elements that constitute a brand's visual identity — logo, colour palette, typography, imagery, composition rules, and application guidelines.

**Why It Matters:** A logo is not an identity. The logo is one element in a system. The system is what makes a brand recognisable across every touchpoint.

**System Components:**
1. **Primary logo** — the main mark
2. **Secondary logos** — variations (horizontal, vertical, simplified)
3. **Symbol** — standalone mark for favicons and small applications
4. **Colour palette** — primary, secondary, and functional colours
5. **Typography system** — headline, body, and accent typefaces
6. **Imagery style** — photography/illustration direction
7. **Composition rules** — grid, spacing, placement
8. **Application examples** — how the system works on real-world items
9. **Voice/tone** — verbal personality matching the visual system
10. **Guidelines** — documentation of how to use (and not use) the system

**The Logo vs. The System:**
- A great logo in a poor system = unrecognisable brand
- A good logo in a great system = recognisable brand
- The system does more work than the logo

**Common Mistakes:**
- Treating the logo as the entire identity
- Designing the logo without considering the system it must live in
- Inconsistent application across touchpoints (the most common identity failure)

**LKG Relationships:** → Consistency (RS-LIC-PH-010), → Meaning (RS-LIC-PH-001), → Authenticity (RS-LIC-PH-007)
